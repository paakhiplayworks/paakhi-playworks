I Built a Number Series Puzzle Game for Android – Here's How It Works

By Nitish Mittal · November 3, 2026

2, 4, 8, 16... what comes next?

If your brain said 32 before you even finished reading, you already know the little thrill this game is built around.

Next Up: Number Series Puzzle is a free Android game by Paakhi Playworks with 100+ levels of number series and pattern puzzles.

This is the story of why I built it, how number series puzzles work, what I learned along the way, and what's coming next from the studio.

It started with my mother

My mother taught me mathematics when I was a kid. Long before school turned it into exams and marks, she made it about patterns and play. That captured my imagination.

Numbers were never a chore. They were puzzles waiting to be cracked.

Years later, during a long stretch when I felt lost, I found my way back to that feeling. Sitting with a problem, working through the logic, and arriving at an answer you can check and trust.

It reminded me how much I love mathematics and logical reasoning, and how much quiet happiness that kind of thinking brings me.

Next Up is my attempt to pass that happiness on. If even a few people open the game and feel a little of what I felt back then, it was worth building.

Why build a thinking game in the age of AI?

Building this game was far easier and more accessible than it would have been a few years ago, and I want to be honest about that.

AI tools helped me throughout the development process, particularly when debugging. Having access to large amounts of technical information and being able to work through problems interactively turned hours of being stuck into minutes.

A solo developer can now build and ship things that would once have required a much larger team.

But that same shift is exactly why I wanted to build this kind of game.

When AI can summarise information, write code and solve problems for us, the ability to think independently matters more, not less.

I don't believe people have stopped thinking. I do believe reasoning is a muscle. It gets sharper with use and duller without it.

I wanted to make something that gives that muscle a workout, while making the workout feel like play rather than homework.

How do number series puzzles work?

A number series puzzle presents a sequence of numbers that follows a hidden mathematical rule. Your job is to identify that rule and work out the next number.

For example:

2, 4, 8, 16, ?

Each number is multiplied by 2. So the answer is 32.

Sounds simple? Not every sequence is that straightforward.

Next Up includes several kinds of number series and mathematical patterns.

1. Arithmetic sequences

These sequences follow a consistent addition or subtraction rule.

Example: 3, 7, 11, 15, ?

Each number increases by 4. The answer is 19.

2. Geometric sequences

Here, each number is multiplied or divided by a fixed number.

Example: 2, 4, 8, 16, ?

Each number is multiplied by 2. The answer is 32.

3. Square number patterns

Some sequences are based on familiar mathematical properties, such as square numbers.

Example: 1, 4, 9, 16, ?

These are the squares of consecutive integers: 1², 2², 3², 4². The answer is 25.

4. Alternating sequences

These are slightly trickier because two different patterns operate within the same sequence.

Example: 1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, ?

Look at the differences: +2, +1, +2, +1, +2. The next step is +1. The answer is 10.

5. Mathematical operations and special number patterns

Not every puzzle follows a simple addition or multiplication rule. Some sequences involve combinations of basic mathematical operations or other interesting number properties.

The fun lies in identifying the underlying logic rather than simply calculating the difference between consecutive numbers.

These are the kinds of patterns that make you pause, look at the sequence again, and occasionally have that satisfying "aha!" moment.

The early levels in Next Up introduce simpler patterns. As you progress, the puzzles become more challenging. Hints are available when you need a little help getting unstuck.

What I learned building Next Up

Next Up was built using Kotlin and Gradle, with Android Studio as the primary development environment. I also used the terminal extensively for builds, debugging and managing the development workflow. AI tools were particularly useful for troubleshooting errors, understanding unfamiliar implementation details and speeding up development.

But getting the game to run was only one part of the journey.

Some of the most challenging work started once there was something to show people.

Finding testers, convincing friends and acquaintances to actually try the game, and getting them to spend enough time with it to uncover obvious bugs was a challenge in itself. Things that seemed perfectly fine during development would sometimes behave differently when someone else played the game.

Then came the practical realities of Android development: making sure the interface worked across different mobile screen sizes, fixing layout issues, updating builds, testing again and repeating the process.

And finally, there was the publishing journey. Preparing a release, submitting it, waiting for the production rollout and making sure everything worked as expected.

One of my biggest learnings was that building a game involves much more than getting the underlying logic right.

A puzzle might be mathematically correct, but that doesn't automatically make it enjoyable.

You have to think about how the player encounters the puzzle, how much information is visible on the screen, when to introduce a hint, and how to make the experience feel rewarding.

Building more than 100 levels also meant thinking beyond individual puzzles. The experience had to feel like a progression, with enough variety to keep players interested.

One thing I learned is that building a game is not just about writing code. It is about taking an idea through several iterations until it becomes something another person can actually enjoy playing.

The first version is rarely the finished product. Getting it into people's hands, listening to feedback and fixing what you missed is just as important as building it in the first place.

What makes a good number series puzzle?

After working on more than a hundred puzzles, there are a few principles I keep coming back to.

1. One clear underlying rule.

A puzzle should reward observation and reasoning. If a player needs to guess what the creator was thinking, the puzzle isn't doing its job.

2. Solvable without becoming trivial.

A good puzzle should be approachable in a minute or two, but not necessarily obvious at first glance. There should be enough challenge to make the player think.

3. The answer should feel earned.

This is probably the most important principle.

Too obvious, and there is no satisfaction. Too obscure, and the player feels cheated.

The sweet spot is a rule that feels almost inevitable once you discover it. You look at the sequence again and wonder how you missed it.

That's the feeling I want Next Up to deliver.
